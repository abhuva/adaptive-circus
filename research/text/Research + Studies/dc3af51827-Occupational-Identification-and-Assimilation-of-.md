ABSTRACT

KEYWORDS

This study examines how professional aerial acrobats are socialized to navigate pain as a feature of their occupational identity. Drawing from qualitative interview and arts-based elicitation data from 27 profes sional aerialists, this study advances organizational communication and body work scholarship by introducing the concept of pain work as a new sub-category of body work. Pain work is a communicative, embodied practice through which workers sustain, endure, and man age pain as a hallmark of occupational identification. The study theo rizes a four-phase cyclical process model of pain work to illustrate how the meaning of pain is co-constructed through organizational assim ilation. Images provided by participants highlight the materialsymbolic interplay of d/Discourse and embodiment. We contribute to broader discussions on the commodification of body work and the need to critically analyze physical conduits of identification at work. Limitations and future directions are discussed.

I think you like pain if you do circus. It’s not like you love pain, but you accept pain to get something that you want to do . . . the pain in the circus has a big, big link – you cannot have one without the other one.

The excerpt above comes from PauPau, a 17-year circus veteran specializing in aerial cradle (i.e., a circus act where a performer hangs by their knees from a metal frame and executes acrobatics by catching, swinging, and tossing another performer). PauPau describes the painful physical toll performers undertake as central to their occupation (i.e., “you accept pain”) and its link to organizational membership (i.e., “pain in the circus . . . you cannot have one without the other one”). Pain, widely defined as the “unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with, or resembling that associated with, actual or potential tissue damage” (Raja et al., 2020), is relative to personal experience and shaped by indivi duals’ background, context, and co-constructions of past pain experiences (Cosio, 2020; Hadjistavropoulos et al., 2004; Linton & Shaw, 2011). Given this definition, pain is a subjective experience. Moreover, the excerpt above illustrates the relative nature of pain and how – in the context of work – pain may shift from an “unpleasant” experience to becoming a desired means of reinforcing occupational identity.

Whereas scholars have analyzed pain as a repercussion of work (e.g., emotional labor and burnout, see Hibbert et al., 2021; Riforgiate et al., 2022), this study is focused on analyzing contexts where pain is a requirement, rather than a byproduct, of work. In doing so, we aim to explore how physical pain is altered – both in the ways in which workers talk about pain and physically endure pain – when workers are highly identified with its sources (e.g., occupational identity and organizational membership; see Harris, 2017; Styhre, 2004). Given that through interaction “symbol becomes material; material becomes symbol; and neither stay the same as a result” (Ashcraft et al., 2009, p. 34), communication about pain both shapes and is shaped by its experience (Hintz, 2020). By examining how pain is experienced at work through the lens of occupational identity and assimilation into an organization, this study redefines pain as a shared, interactional process shaped by sym bolic/material contexts. In doing so, the present study builds upon previous scholars’ efforts to bring to the forefront the elaborate entanglement between discourse and materiality, answering the call to analyze the material, tangible outcomes of discourse on embodiment (see Dougherty et al., 2018; Taylor et al., 2021) Identity, identification, and the body converge in organizing body work (i.e., where the body serves as an organizational resource or facilitates service to others; see Wolkowitz, 2002). Identification “lends order to the body’s raw material resources” and therefore identity is enacted by bodily performance (Ashcraft et al., 2009, p. 32). Situated in an athletic-performative context, this study highlights the tension between the social construc tion of pain and the material limitations of athletes’ bodies. Using in-depth qualitative and arts-based research (ABR; Leavy, 2018), which “combines the tenets of the creative arts in research contexts” (p. 4) and is a useful tool for using art as a form of inquiry, this study captures embodied outcomes of pain at work, allowing participants to express what transcends text alone via photovoice (Wang, 1999; Wang & Burris, 1997). Occupational Identification and Assimilation in a Multi-Organizational Context Occupational identification is a socially constructed process through which individuals “construct their sense of who they are and what they do in relation to their jobs” (Ashcraft, 2007; Meisenbach, 2008, p. 263). Ashcraft (2013) defines occupational identity as “an evolving co-construction of the nature of work, which reflects the embodied identities” of workers (p. 7). The social performance of identity, or identity enactment, is how workers continuously form, strengthen, maintain, or revise their occupational identity (Ashforth et al., 2008). In body work occupations, identification relies on workers’ ability to perform a specific corporeality. Bodies, with their susceptibility to injury, illness, aging, and death, both resist and reflect social construction (Ashcraft et al., 2009; Searle, 1997). By engaging pain as central to occupational identification, our analysis provides insight into how social constructions of the body during assimilation produce material consequences for workers. Organizational assimilation describes how members acquire the norms, rules, behaviors, and expectations of a new organizational culture or role (Jablin, 1987; Kramer & Miller, 1999; Van Maanen & Schein, 1979). Assimilation has four primary stages: (a) anticipatory socialization, (b) organizational entry, (c) ongoing socialization, and (d) organizational exit and disengagement (Berkelaar & Harrison, 2019). Anticipatory socialization, which occurs before joining, includes two sub-stages: (a) vocational anticipatory socialization (i.e., choos ing specific roles) and (b) organizational anticipatory socialization (i.e., selection of

The final stage, exit and disengagement, marks the transition from insider to outsider (Berkelaar & Harrison, 2019). These phases are dynamic and fluid, with blurred boundaries (Kramer, 2011). As mentioned, scholars distinguish between organizational and vocational anticipatory socialization, the latter addressing preparation for specific roles (Kramer, 2011). This study analyzes how workers are socialized into a specific occupational role (i.e., aerial acrobats; a type of circus artist who performs “gymnastic action on and off apparatus suspended in the air”; see Tait, 2005, p. 2) in a multi-organizational context, emphasizing shared norms and expectations of membership as these relate to navigating pain as a feature of work (“the pain in the circus has a big, big link”). In other words, as members assimilate into both a vocation and an organization, they act in ways that demonstrate belonging through identification (e.g., “you like pain if you do circus”; see Bullis & Wackernagel Bach, 1989). While some scholars view identification as a cognitive process (see Mael & Ashforth, 1995), communication scholars emphasize its interactive and social nature (Burke, 1969; Dutton et al., 1994), occurring when members perceive consubstantiality with others (Gossett, 2002). Consubstantiality arises when shared interests, values, and experiences align – as seen in the circus – where individuals feel “both joined and separate” (Burke, 1969, p. 21; Quigley, 1998). For example, aerialists showing off their bruises to peers reflects shared values of hard work and pain, reinforcing identification with the vocation (see Zanin, 2018). How these processes of identification are experienced in relation to navigating pain in aerial acrobatics can be further elucidated by analyzing the interplay of d/Discourse, materiality, and body work. Organizational d/Discourse, Body Work, and Materiality in Circus Alvesson and Kärreman (2000) distinguish between discourse and Discourse. While lowercase “d” discourse attends to the micro-level language use in specific social contexts, uppercase “d” Discourse refers to the larger, dominating uses of language reflective of organiza tional culture and ideologies. This framework assumes organizations are discursively con structed, positing d/Discourse as “the very foundation upon which organizational life is built” (Fairhurst & Putnam, 2004, p. 6). Putnam and Fairhurst (2001) argue that studying language in organizations must go beyond general linguistic approaches of discourse analysis to capture “how discourse processes shape and are shaped by organizational constructs” (p. 4). Concerning the body, Ashcraft et al. (2009) describe communication as an embodied act, where the body is both transformed and constrained by communication. However, while d/Discourses can influence the transformation of the body, bodies and their “performances of identity” (p. 34) remain constrained by their material properties. Embodiment scholarship (see Ellingson, 2017) conceptualizes the body as “the material self that is constructed through interaction with other bodies and material objects” (p. 13). Thus, studying the construction and reproduction of d/Discourse in circus organizations offers insight into how aerialists’ occupational identity emerges through embodied practices. In body work organizations, the body is often managed as an asset to achieve organiza tional goals (e.g., in professional athletics; see Zanin, 2018, 2019). Organizations control members’ bodies by establishing markers for expectations of performance and dedication to

Members who can not meet these expectations – and thereby fail to enact positive occupational identities – may become frustrated with the organization, less engaged, less committed, and less identified as organizational members (Michel, 2011). Consequently, for example, athletes may avoid reporting injuries out of fear of being sidelined (Zanin, 2018), while creative workers enacting a “tortured artist” identity may face higher risks of depression, anxiety, and substance abuse (Alacovska & Kärreman, 2022). This study, therefore, expands upon existing literature on body workers in artistic occupations. Moreover, this study addresses the interplay of d/Discourse, body work, and materiality. Building upon previous literature focusing on the body and organizing, the present study implements ABR to bring to the forefront the material realities of social constructions. Importantly, it addresses the call to centralize materiality beyond what is currently available and expands upon previous attempts to prioritize these efforts. For example, Gist-Mackey and Dougherty (2020) take a critical ethnographical approach to highlight the sociomaterial struggle between embodiment and discourse in an unemployment support organization. Dougherty et al. (2018) employ a discursive-material framework to examine how the unemployed manage food (in)security through the use of ABR. Along a similar vein, Ivancic and Dooling (2023) analyze stigmatizing discourses of food assistance programs through a sociomaterial lens, highlighting how the interplay of discourse and materiality cocreate shame. Lastly, Dougherty (2011) centralizes the material-discursive dialectic by underscoring the material consequences of discourse in class structures, power imbalances, and the material-realities on farmers and their work. Taken together, these efforts elucidate the need to bring more attention to the body in organizing and how materiality plays a role in these dynamics. In their journal forum (see Taylor et al., 2021), Ellingson highlights embodied ethnography as linking “the material and discursive as joint foci in research” (p. 636); an effort we attempt to build upon here by showcasing the material-realities of the body at work and how these are co-created through discursive norms. Particularly, in terms of body work, this study highlights how the body and organizing interact in terms of materializing pain through discourse in a performative-athletic context. In circus organizations, the “essence of circus” (Davis, 2018) involves pushing the body’s material boundaries. Aerialists, in particular, are expected to awe and entertain by blending strength and agility (Tait, 2005). They exercise intense upper-body and core muscularity while portraying agility and grace. Aerial acrobats perform “physical prowess and innova tion” (Calver, 2020, p. 308), often displaying seemingly superhuman abilities developed by an intense training schedule (see O’Donnell, 2023). However, these feats of strength and agility come at a tangible, material cost to workers. Circus scholars have documented the ubiquity of pain in the aerialist occupational identity. An interview study of 31 aerialists noted, “the shocks absorbed by the aerialists’ bodies flying high above crowds would rupture most humans” (Walby & Stuart, 2021, p. 7) and “circus aerialists often work through pain that would overwhelm the average human” (p. 15). Like other professional athletes (see Zanin, 2018, 2019), aerialists confront and exceed material limitations (Legendre, 2016), yet they are limited by their biological properties (Ashcraft et al., 2009). Method The corpus of data for this analysis comes from a larger project on body work in circus arts. After completing the necessary procedures for approval, participants were recruited through representative and snowball sampling. Representative sampling enabled the recruitment of participants that “replicat[ed] characteristics of the larger group” (Tracy, 2020, p. 83). The first author’s seven-year membership in circus arts organizations facili tated recruitment via direct communication, messaging applications, and social media. Inclusion criteria required participants to: (a) be 18 or older, (b) have at least two years of aerial acrobatics experience, (c) self-identify as an aerialist, (d) train within a circus orga nization (i.e., not self-taught), and (e) have coaching or performing experience in a circus organization. Participants completed a demographic survey and two interviews, with the second interview including an ABR component. All interviews were conducted via Zoom, with the exception of two in-person. Interviews were both video and audio recorded with participants’ consent. Most primary and secondary interviews were conducted within one week of each other. Participants received compensation for participating in each interview portion of the study (i.e., the equivalent of $25 USD for each interview portion). Participants A total of 27 circus acrobats participated in this study. Participant ages ranged from 19 to 47 years (M = 30.11). Full demographic information is included in Table 1. Participants’ experience in circus acrobatics ranged from 3 to 23 years (M = 10.11). All participants received formal training through an established circus organization, school, or program and had previously coached and/or performed aerial acrobatics. At the time of data collection, participants reported they currently coached and performed (n = 20), only coached (n = 2), only performed (n = 1), or were not currently coaching or performing due to COVID-19 restrictions and shutdowns (n = 4). Participants provided a variety of specialized training in their apparatus(es) of choice and declared multiple specialties (see Table 1). The interview process was twofold: a primary interview and a secondary interview with the inclusion of photo elicitation. Data Analysis Guided by abductive reasoning (Charmaz, 2014), data analysis followed an iterative process that incorporated research questions, emergent data, and guiding literature (Tracy, 2020). After transcribing and reviewing interviews, the first author conducted line-by-line coding of 20% of the transcripts, as suggested by Tracy (2020), to identify emergent themes concerning past literature on assimilation and body work. Continuing a constant compara tive method for emergent codes, the first author identified preliminary themes regarding pain and injury, particularly during participants’ discussion of socialization practices. All mentions of pain and injury were included during this phase. For example, participants’ mention of how their coaches responded to pain-related expressions was coded as sepa rately from their own coaching approach when responding to students’ expressions of pain. When data did not fit into an established code, we created a new code. This process continued until the pain and injury-related data were coded. Next, the first author grouped emergent codes into different categories (e.g., “good pain” and “bad pain”). In vivo terms (i.e., using direct phrases from participants; Tracy, 2020) were used to label some specific codes (i.e., “circus hurts”). Third, as part of the iterative process, emergent codes were categorized into instances related to anticipatory socialization, ongoing socialization, identification, and members’ everyday talk. Through this secondary-cycle coding process (Tracy, 2020), the emergent coding categories were reviewed for overlap and theoretical fit to provide interpretations and explanations of the data. These coding categories were then contrasted and refined, revealing different phases of socialization when navigating pain. This analysis revealed pain

Photovoice Analysis Using Capous-Desyllas and Bromfield’s (2018) arts-informed approach to photovoice data analysis, the first author created a codebook addendum that included participants’ images and reviewed each image alongside their transcribed voiceover text. Findings The findings reveal pain work as a unique subcategory of body work in which workers are required to sustain, endure, and manage pain to fulfill their occupational role. Building upon body work scholarship that centralizes different aspects of work that people do with their bodies, such as dirty work (Ashforth & Kreiner, 1999), risk work (Walby & Stuart, 2021), blood work (Sang et al., 2021), or endurance work (McNarry et al., 2020), this study situates pain as a medium through which workers learn to meet the standards of their occupational role. How pain workers meet the criteria to embody their occupational identity is demonstrated through a cyclical process detailing the iterative transformation of pain: (a) experience, (b) tolerate, (c) embrace, and (d) proselytize (see Figure 5). The integration of a photovoice methodology further accentuates the vivid embodied experience of the pain these workers undergo and both the visible and invisible corporeal outcomes of their occupational expectations. Pain, as a feature of occupational identification to becoming a performer and assimilation into organizational membership, was introduced early in participants’ experiences. The following sections detail the process of pain reconceptualization through four cyclical phases: (a) experience, (b) tolerate, (c) embrace, and (d) proselytize (see Figure 5). Experience Phase When introduced to aerial acrobatics, newcomers navigate sensations of pressing flesh against metal or fabric to sustain their body weight. These initial sensory experiences can be intense and disconcerting. During her first aerial class, for example, straps artist Stella recalled, “It hurt so much to even go on the ropes and hold on to the bar.” This apparatusbody connection is visually represented in Figure 1, provided by rope (i.e., corde lisse) artist Juliet. In her image, one can see the cotton-wrapped nylon rope digging into Juliet’s back. She chose this image to let people know that “there’s some pain involved with the rope” even when she appears “daring,” “artistic,” and “strong.” While Juliet is a seasoned perfor mer, her image reveals the persistent, inherent tension of relying on an apparatus that continually exerts pressure against the body. For newcomers, this pressure is particularly taxing. Silks artist Charlie shared early experiences on the lyra: “What felt painful was just the metal hoop on my pelvis, or in my hands.” Here, Charlie’s account reveals how newcomers are not only greeted by pain, but often suspended in pain. Importantly, participants revealed that there was little to no mention of pain prior to the first time they climbed onto an apparatus. The lack of forewarning of pain sensations positions newcomers as having to navigate the surprise of pain, often while on the apparatus itself, setting up a dynamic of relying on the coach to make sense of their own experience; not from a subjective perspective, but rather, from a third-party (i.e., the coach). Thus, the meaning-making experience of pain is marked by a slight but significant linguistic shift. Language Shifts from “Pain” To “Discomfort”. Reframing pain as discomfort is pivotal to the identification and assimilation process. This linguistic shift invites newcomers to accept pain as temporary. Silks artist Emily shares, “I know a lot of people really don’t like using the word pain . . . I’m going to reword it to discomfort.” While “pain” carries negative connota tions, “discomfort” is less alienating. This reframing aligns with Ashcraft et al.’s (2009) position on the body as a communicative product and the corporeal effects of discourse about the body. Lyra and sling artist Tia shares, “I think the word pain, we need to be careful . . . Because it’s just your brain reacting to a new sensation, and it’s trying to protect you . . . It’s just uncomfortable because it’s new, and my body doesn’t like it . . . it’s discomfort, right?” Aerialists reframe pain through language to internalize that they are not in danger, enabling them to endure the discomfort. Pain Typology and Setting Boundaries. Having accepted pain as tolerable, an impor tant feature of the pain reconceptualization process in pain work is to distinguish between “productive pain” and “destructive pain.” Emerging as an in vivo code, “productive pain” is defined as pain that is temporary or fulfills a purpose (i.e., “acclimation” pain, hurts “in the moment,” or “surface pain”). Whereas productive pain is normalized and expected, destructive pain impairs mobility or can lead to injury. Participants described destructive pain as “deeper, more intense,” more in line with “sharp stabs, burning, radiating pain,” “the kind of pain where something is not right,” or “pain that causes harm.” As a way of example, straps artist Ellie expressed, “The more you put your body through, the more you push yourself physically, you’re going to start to find those lines.” Here, Ellie describes her integration into pain work as a learned, continuous process of identification. To navigate the line between productive and destructive pain, she must repeatedly experience both, allowing her to discern between them and establish boundaries around what she is willing – and able – to endure. Another example comes from trapeze artist Frodo, who broke her leg during a performance when the tree branch she was rigged onto simply broke off. She shared, When I broke my leg . . . it was obvious for me that I wouldn’t be able to continue the performance. But when it comes to open hands, your skin burns, bruises . . . It’s actually about this process of teaching your body that even though it might be a bit painful now, you’re not gonna die . . . it’s more about learning to be able to put boundaries for yourself, what is actually too much and where is okay.

While Frodo seemingly enacts agency over the pain she is willing to endure (e.g., “learning to be able to put boundaries for yourself”), breaking her leg instantly precludes her from performing (i.e., bodily resistance to organizational identities; see Ashcraft et al., 2009). Interviewees echoed Frodo’s sentiment and offered statements such as “I can handle the superficial pains really easily on the skin . . . it’s just part of it,” and that they need to know the difference between, “my body will not go there versus it doesn’t want to go there.” In the latter statement, the body not wanting to go there is indicative of productive pain you can and should push through, thereby reinforcing pain work expectations. Although navigating pain boundaries should be a personal decision, the process is mediated by the normative expectations of performers’ occupation, illustrating how work contexts can influence the conceptualization of pain when pain is embedded into the occupational identity. Whereas newcomers initially rely on their subjective, first-person verified experience of pain, they learn to filter and reframe their embodied experience through an objective, third-person verification process. For example, when coaches cate gorize students’ pain as “productive,” this discursive interaction constitutes a meaningmaking process that transforms how pain is perceived by the pain recipient. In other words, coaches’ categorization of pain as “productive” reshapes participants’ understanding of pain. Taken together, the subjective, individual experience of embodied pain is influenced by the co-construction of a social reality of how pain should be experienced.

Embrace Phase With modified attitudes, aerialists learn to value pain as a measure of skill and growth. Lyra artist Elisa shares how she associates pride and accomplishment with pain: You’ve got to embrace it . . . there’s a level of enjoyment with the pain and with this idea of pushing your body to its limits. Elisa reproduces her association between pride and pain through her coaching. The senior student who is praised by their coach for enduring pain and achieving a skill has their subjective embodied experience qualified by an external party as a source of pride. Proselytize Phase Visible lesions from training, such as bruises and burns, become symbols of membership and occupational identification. Participant responses indicate these lesions are symboli cally constructed as membership artifacts, inviting others to normalize and exalt pain as well. This process is a collaborative one as aerialists “show off their battle scars” to “earn their badassery” or praise the visual lesions of others. Silks artist JaJa shared an anecdote where her nine-year-old student approached her with “the biggest smile” and said, “Coach! Look at my hands!” JaJa shared this image, in which you can see the child’s peeling calluses. Lyra artist Jo shared, “We would get so proud of our bruises and our silk burns . . . and there was a little bit of a machismo around like look how beat up I am.” Statements from interviewees indicate this visual exchange often resulted in bonding with other performers and thereby bolstered their collective identity in their valuation of pain. During a trial week to join a new circus school, rope artist Juliet relays the following encounter, “I got a really big rip and I went to get it bandaged up and someone from a different specialization was like, ‘Oh, welcome!,’” indicating that she was accepted as an member of the ingroup. Juliet underscores the symbolic meaning of abrasions as a measure of identification. She notes, “It’s almost a mark of, you’re an aerialist . . . and of course sometimes they weren’t fun, but it was kind of fun to be in a group and like, ‘Oh, man, that’s a nasty burn.’” Similarly, silks artist Lauren “fondly” remembers a playful competition with her friends in which they all shared pictures of their bruises. Lauren was “crowned the winner of the worst bruises,” and her friends joked that her prize was a frozen bag of peas. These instances of consubstantiality through visual abrasions were juxtaposed with instances in which aerialists experienced frustration from a lack of abrasions. Lyra artist Elisa, for example, shared: There’s a kind of pride in the damage that they do to their bodies. If you’re gonna do something hard, you want to have a bruise or leave a mark. I know personally I don’t bruise easily and it’s frustrating when I have under the skin kind of pain that you can’t see. It was so hard, like “You don’t believe me?” Where other kids that’ll come in and be like, “Look at my arm! My whole arm’s purple!”

Elisa’s excerpt indicates how her inability to show her abrasions inhibited her performance of occupational identity, an example of how materiality resists social construction. In this case, however, the resistance comes from the absence of visible bruises. When pain is imbued into an occupational role, workers may perceive an identity threat when unable to visually demonstrate how much pain they are sustaining. This dynamics brings up ethical considerations of the psychological and physical implications that these ideologies may have upon all workers and especially on young, impressionable, and marginalized performers. As a way of consideration, an up-and-coming aerialist may resort to more extreme measures of pain exposure to demonstrate a stronger identification than another performer, particularly in a competitive work environment within a saturated job market. While beyond the scope of the current study, these findings underscore potential implications of the embodied outcomes of these discourses on body workers and should be further explored in future research. All in all, we find the material representation of pain and subsequent valorization of pain as indicative of in-group membership delineates how pain work norms are (re) produced both discursively (i.e., “You love pain if you are an aerialist”) and materially (i.e., demonstrating visible corporeal effects in the way of bruises, abrasions, etc.).

An example of an “apparatus kiss” is depicted in Figure 4, provided by trapeze artist Frodo in response to the study prompt: Provide an image that represents the reality of being an aerialist. By using the metaphor of “kisses,” participants modify the meaning of an abrasion from damaging to desirable and indicative of affection; thereby bolstering the shared social reality that pain is desirable. Moreover, when asked about common tropes they would hear around their circus school or from coaches and other members, participants (re)produced these as axioms. Some of these common phrases included, “If you shower after training and it doesn’t hurt, then you haven’t worked hard enough [because you won’t feel your burns],” “If it doesn’t hurt, you’re not doing it right,” “No pain, no gain,” “Circus hurts, suck it up buttercup,” and referring to a lyra as the “hoop of hurt.” One respondent shared that at her studio, members would refer to Tylenol as “Circus candy” because of how often members needed to consume painkillers. She would say [loudly], “Circus what?!” and the kids would go “Circus hurts!”

At this workplace, Barbara’s recollection of the “Circus hurts!” chant perpetuated by the owner and reproduced by impressionable students solidified the reconceptualization of the experience of pain as a seemingly badge of honor and collective identity formation. The phrase, “Circus hurts” was often shared as a hashtag (i.e., #circushurts) when participants posted pictures of themselves to their social media accounts with bruises, burns, etc. or videos of themselves falling or having a painful mishap while doing aerial acrobatics. Discussion Through a qualitative analysis of interview data from 27 professional acrobats, this study offers key insights into occupational identification, organizational assimilation, and body work scholarship. We document the communicative, embodied processes through which aerial acrobats are socialized to navigate pain as a feature of their occupational identity, highlighting the tangible, material-realities of discourse through the ABR method of photovoice. This section outlines the study’s primary contributions: (a) the concept of pain work as a new sub-category of body work and (b) a process model of assimilation through embodied pain in occupational identification. More broadly, we call for a critical analysis of physical conduits of identification at work, emphasizing pain as a key analytical tool for understanding how such conduits shape identification. A New Sub-Category of Body Work: Pain Work The findings reveal that aerial acrobats undergo occupational assimilation processes that reshape how they engage with pain. Pain, when central to occupational identity, is theorized as pain work—a sub-category of body work (Wolkowitz, 2002) – characterized by specific socialization processes, consequences, and potential benefits for workers. While previous scholarship related to body work has centralized various aspects of work (see Ashforth & Kreiner, 1999; Sang et al., 2021), our findings situate pain as a physical conduit for identification through which workers make sense of their occupational roles and expecta tions. We do not claim that aerialists are pain workers alone, given that they would also qualify as endurance (McNarry et al., 2020) and risk workers (Walby & Stuart, 2021). However, the present study highlights pain as a mechanism for positive job performance as well as a necessary feature for workers’ ability to fulfill their occupational role. Pain workers, in order to sustain, endure, and manage embodied pain to perform their occupational roles, come to understand their experience of pain through d/Discourse. For instance, veteran aerialists teach newcomers to reframe “pain” as “discomfort” (i.e., through everyday talk; little “d” discourse). In doing so, the external attribution of an internal subjective experience (re)produces big “D” discourses that commodify embodied pain (e.g., “the pain in the circus has a big, big link – you cannot have one without the other one”). Pain is integral to occupational identification, enabling pain workers to enact positive identity through pain and align their behavior accordingly (Dutton et al., 1994). Pain,

therefore, serves as a gatekeeper for identification: “If people can’t deal with pain, circus isn’t probably for them.” Aerialists claim consubstantiality by (re)producing d/Discourses normalizing pain as integral to the aerialist identity (Burke, 1969; Gossett, 2002). For example, they claim oneness with the aerialist identity when showing off bruises as these demonstrate an “acting-together” through observable material evidence, as underscored by participants’ responses to both interview questions and ABR prompts. Aspiring aerial acrobats experience initial pain when climbing onto a new apparatus and before they can try any tricks (e.g., “If it doesn’t hurt you, you’re doing it wrong”). Through occupational assimilation (Berkelaar & Harrison, 2019; Jablin, 1987; Kramer & Miller, 1999), participants learn to interpret pain as an avenue for occupational identification. Ongoing socialization (re)produces pain work d/Discourses that transform how workers experience pain. Within pain work, pain becomes an occupational norm, and workers develop heuristics through everyday talk to navigate it socially and experientially. These findings carry broader implications of pain work beyond the current occupational context. Building upon this novel concept of pain work, future scholarship can extend to other artistic-athletic professions as well as performative contexts (e.g., dance, figure skating, burlesque, etc.), blue-collar work (i.e., professions where manual labor is centralized), the military, and sex work, to name a few.

Pain Work Socialization and Identification Process Model The second key contribution is a theorized model of how the meaning-making of pain occurs through organizational assimilation. This four-phase model involves: (a) experience, (b) tolerate, (c) embrace, and (d) proselytize (see Figure 5). This embodied socialization process forms part of the ongoing socialization stage of assimilation into an occupational culture (Jablin, 1987; Kramer & Miller, 1999; Van Maanen & Schein, 1979). Through discourse, members learn their organizational culture’s norms expectations (see Dutton et al., 1994), enabling the co-construction of pain and its evolving meaning in occupational identification. First, pain work socialization processes are more effective when individuals perceive agency over their pain rather than experiencing imposed-upon pain. Zanin (2018) notes that athletes often exercise pseudo-agency, exerting control over structures while reinfor cing hegemonic ones. Similarly, the findings of the present study indicate participants’ ability to choose their pain enabled them to exert control over their pain work, as their pain was simultaneously commodified for their organizations’ benefit. These findings align with Tracy and Tretheway’s (2005) concept of auto-dressage, where members self-discipline to align with preferred occupational identities. Pain work commodifies pain while demonstrating work ers’ commitment to occupational norms. Second, the findings illuminate the understudied stage of ongoing socialization by displaying the cyclical nature of socialization in a pain work context. Aerialists revisit earlier assimilation stages when learning new skills or apparatuses, indicating that occupational roles continually evolve. Within circus organizations, given the wealth of training possibi lities, the potential for skill development ensures that aerialists – and circus performers in general – are never fully actualized in their roles.

Third, the model emphasizes how d/Discourse shapes workers’ symbolic and physiolo gical conceptualizations of pain. This addresses Ashcraft et al.’s (2009) call to explore interactions between discourse and materiality in organizational contexts. Symbolic con structions are limited by material realities, as evidenced when excessive pain results in injury, impairing mobility, and identification. Building upon previous scholars who have pioneered these efforts (see Dougherty, 2011; Gist-Mackey Dougherty, 2020), our findings contribute to a nuanced understanding of the material-symbolic interplay of the body at work, showing how d/Discourses reframe pain without eliminating its material conse quences – as evidenced by the images shared by pain workers. Fourth, this study highlights the implications of the physical-symbolic relationship for organizational management of workers’ bodies (Harris, 2017). By redefining pain as a coconstructed, embodied identification process, we underscore pain as a mechanism for understanding physical conduits of identification (e.g., such as hazing or pain valorization). Given the potential ramifications of pain experiences to the wellbeing of workers and as integral to identification, we urge scholars to critically examine other physical identification conduits at work. Moreover, agency over pain – where individuals choose their pain – has broader implications for rationalizing pain in occupational settings (e.g., boxing, ballet) and in health contexts where patients manage pain (Turner et al., 2005). To better guide these efforts, and with a better understanding of how pain work processes manifest in this occupational context, the findings of this study can inform future interventions in the way of helping to craft organizational policies, training regiments, injury and recovery protocols, and occupational sustainability and longevity efforts for performers by providing empirically-based education that benefit both workers and employers. We encourage future communication scholarship on these demographics to collaborate with sports medicine scholars and providers to bolster the benefits of integrating empirically-informed commu nication practices and strategies that may lead to overall improved wellbeing for pain workers while maintaining occupational expectations for performance. Furthermore, the findings of this study as they relate to the interactional co-constructed process of the lived experience of pain, how pain is approached, and how pain is managed, can be extended to more deeply engage with complementary interdisciplinary approaches to pain such as medical anthropology, sports medicine, and public health. Lastly, the nuanced interaction of pain and occupational identification is made promi nent via the ABR portion of data collection and analysis. In this regard, the images provided by pain workers display vivid imagery of the material consequences of pain as a conduit for occupational identification. We expand upon efforts to use ABR as a tool of inquiry in the context of occupational identification and analyzing material realities made prominent via communication in organizational contexts (see Dougherty et al., 2018; Sharafizad et al., 2020).
