PROP SWAP NOTATION STRUCTURE ARRANGEMENTS The Juggle Board has two primary forms of arrangement activity: FORWARD and LATERAL. Different sets of numeric and alphabetic labels are used to indicate which arrangement is being described by notation. Forward Arrangements use numeric reference of 1-5 and Lateral Arrangements use alphabetic reference of A-E.

TIMINING Reading notation works on the same principal as following a timeline. Notes represent single beats within a pattern and every beat assumes to alternate one side to the other. This means that different sides of the body must take turns in performing actions, unless otherwise notated by something else.

ORBIT Notation is writing as the shortest segment of a repeating pattern. This means it assumes to repeat. Note that for Even-Cycle patterns the hands do not change position; however, that for Odd-Cycle patterns the hands rotate position from one repetition to the next.

START POSITIONS Start order is determined by whichever side the first notation is located and favours proximity to the same side of the body. This means that patterns should begin from the side of the body that is closest and does not cross-over to have to reach the note. This avoids adding unintended complexity to simpler activity; however, there are circumstance where crossing-over is intended and notated separately. Note that for Forward Arrangement patterns the start position is determined by the first notation within the pattern. However, in Lateral Arrangement notation applies to both sides, so there is no set order to starting positions. In this situation the starting hand is decided by choice and alternates in either direction from this point forward. Forward Example (asynchronous): 2345

REVERSE Revere notations invert the standard order of actions within a pattern. Reverse action is indicated using the letter x and is applied in one of two ways: to the pattern as whole or individually to specific notes within the pattern. In the first case of reversing the effect of an entire pattern the x symbol appears before notation, followed by a period, to indicate its overall effect. This applies to asynchronous tempos only. Reverse Example (asynchronous): x.23

! In other situations such as with synchronous activity, the reversing of orders must be indicated more clearly for each beat of notation. In this case individual x symbols are used to indicate each specific action of change. Beginning from the standard assumption of none crossing action, any notation of x instructs the crossing or uncrossing of sides. Synchronous crossing action causes sides to overlap in a way that one side goes over and the other side goes under the other. Note that alternating top and bottom crossing sides has great impact on changing the level of challenge. For this reason the notation assumes the order of the first note in a synchronous set to be on the top-side. In the case of (x23) the side that reaches for the 2 is ordered on top, whereas (x32) directs the side that reaches for the 3 to be in the top order. Forward Reverse (synchronous): (x23)(x45)

OPEN-STATE Activity in which the location of objects changes lanes as part of their event orbits. This condition is marked by the enclosure of square brackets along with a dash mark, used to indicate the transitioning state from one location to another. For example, the Fixed-State pattern of 2345 can be transposed into an Open-State variation by grouping the actions of each hand and assigning them a relationship of transference. Written as an Open-State pattern 2345 transfers into [2~4][3~5]. The enclosed bracket of [2~4] indicates that the object in the 2 position is transferred to the 4 position before performing the action, which in this case is to roll the ball. The second set of enclosed brackets mirrors the first set, but in this case it is performed with the opposite hand on the other side of the board. MIXED-STATE Mixed-State activity combines the events of Fixed-State and Open-State in combination. This State of activity triggers what is referred to as disassociated signalling, as the cognitive signal required to perform each task are different and there by require more accurate precise sequencing capacity. These activities are considered advanced both in technical execution and comprehension. The complexity of applied order makes Mixed-State patterns difficult to explain and share with none verbal populations, so it is considered not fully accessible and rarely included in base routines of functionally diverse individuals. The rules of notation continue to apply in the same way for Mixed-State as for all other States. The thing here is to pay close attention and workout notations slowly. The closed parenthesis in the first examples indicates synchronous tempo and the internal hard brackets indicate that there are two forms of action, there by making it Mixed-State. Graded Tempos The Graded Tempo Chart is a resource tool that helps understand and apply different states of tempo to individual participant needs. Each roll of a ball back and forth between facilitators and participants is considered to be a single obit of the event. However, there are sub periods within the single orbit of each event and the tracking and grading of each of these periods helps in setting and progressing individual participants Zone of Proximal development. The images in the chart below indicate at which period stage to release of send the next event in the sequence. The 0level allows all objects to return to their original positions, before sending the next event. This allows participants to follow events with their eyes, after releasing the ball and sending it back to the facilitator. For very young people or individuals with cognitive delays, becoming aware of new events while still watching the last events motion can present barriers. The capacity to develop here is eye-tracking and it can be improved by gradually introducing the next events slightly earlier and earlier than the point when the ball returns to the pocket and completes its orbit. So, level-1 tempo is where we can develop eye-tracking and response time. The next Graded Tempo level is that which we call the “Base Tempo”. This is the state of tempo, when there is no overlap between the participants completion of a motor task and the next incoming event, but also there are no gaps in processing time. There is always something for the participant to be managing at this tempo. Another important element of this tempo is that it can easily be set by the participant naturally, without having to prompt them to do anything. It becomes there natural rhythm and tempo, which by a facilitator is able to mimic and stimulate mirroring behaviour in the participant.

From the Base Tempo, participants are easily guided and responsive to the suggestive influences of facilitator. Progressing the tempo beyond that of the Base Temp can be a more difficult challenge when working in Open-State patterns. In these situations participants must pickup, move, place, and roll each event, prior to or at the same time as processing multiple other events. An indicator that the tempo is beyond the participants Zone of Proximal Development is when any motor control movements show hesitation or pause in response to managing sequenced movements. Adjusting the tempo up and down in regard to the Graded Tempo Chart assist in managing these transitions.

Process Formations The Process Formations Chart is another resource tool that enables clear intentional development of participant sequencing capacity. The chart is designed in consideration of the three sequencing factors present in all juggling coordinations: Timing, Spatial Orientation, and Processing Signal. The timing of a juggling sequence can exist as either synchronous or asynchronous. The spatial orientation can be either Lateral or Bi-Lateral, and the Processing Signals of each action can be either Associated or Disassociated.

! The chart lays out the eight possible combinations of juggling sequencing capacity in order of difficulty or challenge. The first being the easiest to perform or containing the least amount of cross-hemisphere communication tasks involved in the process. Note that with Associated Signals the synchronous state of action precedes the asynchronous state in terms of accessibility. This is due to the fact that the brain is not required to split the Associated Signals of each action, so it is able to consolidate the signal of both sides into a single synchronous tempo. In the state of Disassociated Signalling, the Asynchronous Tempo precedes the Synchronous Tempo in accessibility as it allows separation of motor control signals to be organised in alternating tempos.

Primary Composition The Primary Composition is a standard model of introductory activity that is intended to by performed in continuous sequence without any pause in activity. It is best to think of it as a choreography or a dance. The progressions of the Primary Composition are based on neurological, phycological, and mathematical study of the juggling experience. It deviates from standard learning models of juggling in that it focuses more on the cognitive training elements of the task, rather than the technical motor control movements. It is important to understand the value of tempo in creating the optimal learning experience for participants. In order for the most natural learning experience to take place, the facilitator must take on the responsibility of invite and encourage participants into a flow-like state of mind. This is achieved through prolonged uninterrupted rhythmic exercise of increasing complexity. The below guide of the Primary Composition includes notes about common obstacles and barriers to focus on, and useful tips on how to overcome each step of the way.
