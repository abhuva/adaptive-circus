# Cases and contrasts

## Running tic-tac-toe · practitioner account

Marc described roughly eight children aged about eight or nine, with varied support needs. Two teams ran to a three-by-three grid to place or reposition their coloured balls. The children ran willingly. Placements appeared largely random to the educators, and the team stopped the game. This is a retrospective group account. The observations do not establish what each child understood.

**What the team did.** They sat together and used the remaining fifteen minutes or so for sorting colours, matching objects to coloured plates, and making lines. They repeated those activities the following day and tried the game again. Marc reports that it went much better. The educators’ interpretation was that they had assumed several prerequisites at once.

**What an AI proposed before hearing the outcome.** Completing the run and releasing the next runner gave immediate feedback even for a tactically poor move. Turning to run back also took a child’s eyes off the board just when the consequences of the move could become interesting. The AI proposed starting one move before a win and having the children direct an adult runner. This was an alternative interpretation and an untried design.

**What remains open.** The successful adaptation changed colour and line practice, pace, viewpoint, social arrangement and familiarity. It supplies no isolated estimate of any one change. A future contrast, if it fits the group’s purpose, would be to let a player stay beside the grid and direct a runner. Choosing a useful move under those conditions would inform the visibility or time-pressure explanation. Difficulty choosing one would still admit several explanations. No child needs to repeat a frustrating version to settle the question.

## Juggleboard group passing · practitioner account

Marc’s group exploration has mainly involved capable adults. Players synchronize, pass, create patterns and invent movements. The board constrains trajectories and makes some forms of group passing easier. This supports a description of adult creative practice. Outcomes for children with disabilities in this group format remain open.

**Proposed contrast.** After a missed exchange, have a partner wait for the player’s restart cue, or have everyone follow a fixed count. Either arrangement could produce a good duet. Only the first gives that player control of when play resumes, provided the partner really waits. Comparing what players say they attended to can reveal differences invisible in a count of completed passes.

## Clown duet · hypothetical design

A performer wants to shape the laugh in a double take. Their partner can manipulate the prop and perform a large turn. The performer chooses when to cue the second look. Rehearse with two different pauses and ask the performer which they want in the act. If the partner starts the turn on a memorized count regardless of the cue, the performer has lost control over that piece of timing even when the gesture and the laugh look much the same.

This example illustrates a design question. No participant outcome is claimed. A wish to perform in this way is sufficient reason to keep the support. Training independent turns would be a different purpose.
