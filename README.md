# Adaptive circus pedagogy

**Working question. For this person or group, what can we change so they can engage with the challenge that matters to them?**

This is a first-pass framework for educators designing or revising a circus activity. It grew out of Marc Bielert’s inclusive circus practice and a discussion with an AI collaborator in November 2026. The categories and proposed tests are open for revision. They describe situations. They do not assign people a level or a diagnosis.

## Three design decisions

1. **Choose a purpose and say whose it is.** A participant may want to perform with a friend while an educator wants to explore recovery after a missed beat. Either can be a reasonable purpose. Ask which one should guide this particular adaptation. A session for enjoyment needs no further developmental justification.
2. **Name the demand to keep and the demand to change.** “Improve timing” leaves too much open. “Notice a partner is late and choose when to restart” describes something a person can actually practise. A ball that drops and rolls away might obstruct that opportunity. A tether can make a missed beat recoverable while leaving the tempo quick.
3. **Locate control and agency.** Who perceives the cue, decides, acts, and repairs an interruption? What do a partner and the apparatus take care of? If the participant pauses, does anything wait for them? Control describes what their action changes. Agency also concerns whose intention can change the activity. Someone else may carry out a movement that the participant directs.

Use observation on either side of these decisions. Before adapting, describe the event closely enough that two educators could offer different explanations. Afterwards, choose evidence appropriate to the purpose. Seeing someone do something today establishes access under those conditions. Seeing it again later helps assess learning. Seeing it in a different task helps assess transfer. A participant’s account can tell us what the experience meant to them.

## Ways to vary an activity

| Demand | Examples of changes an educator or participant can make |
|---|---|
| Movement and support | Alter reach, force, balance or precision. Use a plane to constrain a ball’s path, a partner to carry part of the load, or breath to initiate movement. |
| Space and time | Change distance, rhythm, timing window or what happens after a missed beat. Slowing the rhythm and making a miss recoverable can have different effects. |
| Perception and decisions | Make the relevant cue easier to distinguish, show the outcome, reduce concurrent choices, or keep the board visible after a move. |
| Coordination with others | Share initiation, vary who leads, or let a player wait, decline, repair or propose a variation. |
| Expression and exposure | Try an idea in private, choose the audience, delegate a movement, or let the performer decide the duration of a comic pause. |

These dimensions interact. A partner adds another person to follow and may also take over the most demanding catch. When the purpose is access, an effective support can stay in the finished act.

## Relation to functional juggling and social circus

Craig Quat’s distinction between expression and experience makes it possible to ask which relationship survives a change of apparatus. His categories of space, time, and signal describe options for composing activities. For example, matching and differing movements of the two hands are distinguishable in both simultaneous and alternating patterns. The label alone leaves open how the demand changes for a particular player. Propswap notation captures relationships that a facilitator can overlook, such as an odd-length sequence changing hands on repetition. Packeting moves gradually from simultaneous to alternating arrivals inside a rhythm already shared.

Marc reports over seven years of successful use and adaptation. His criticism concerns the book’s accessibility, its certainty about neurological mechanisms, and its treatment of scaffolding as a fixed linear progression. Adjusting demands responsively is itself an established form of scaffolding. A score can describe a pattern exactly while leaving the participant’s experience uncertain.

Social circus may ask what participants can experience, express, or change together. Adaptive circus pedagogy asks how to arrange an activity for a particular purpose. The questions overlap in a rehearsal. A relative discovering a child’s comic timing at a show is a change in the relative’s perception. It need not imply a change in the child’s motor capacity.
